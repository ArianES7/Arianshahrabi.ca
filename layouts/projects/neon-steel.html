Project Overview

What Is Neon Steel

Neon Steel is a 2.5D action beat ’em up made by a 20-person team at BloodFeather Studios. I worked as Executive Producer and UI/UX Developer, splitting my time between leading production and building the game’s interface systems.

I led the team through development and release. I planned milestones, managed scope, tracked blockers, coordinated playtests, and worked with the other leads to keep development moving.

I also worked directly on the interface. I organized the HUD information, gave the Neon Steel art team feedback and direction, and built the HUD and menu systems in Unreal Engine with Blueprints.

We released the downloadable Windows build on June 19, 2026. Future updates are planned.

Production and Workflow

How I Led Production

The team covered programming, design, art, animation, audio, narrative, and UI. With 20 people involved, much of my work came down to making sure each discipline knew what it needed, what was blocking it, and what had to happen next.

I planned milestones, set priorities, tracked dependencies, followed up on blockers, ran reviews, and coordinated playtests. When the scope changed, I worked with the other leads to compare each feature against the time and people we had available.

We protected the features needed for release. The team reached feature complete on June 5, two weeks before launch. We used the remaining time for marketing, bug fixing, and playtesting.

Story Beats Board

The Story Beats Board mapped what happened throughout the game, what the player would encounter, and when new elements appeared.

The narrative and art teams completed the board with input from Toronto Film School faculty. Narrative work had its own section, while the rest of the board showed how the story and gameplay progressed alongside each other.

I reviewed the pacing and paid close attention to when Kade gained new combat abilities. Kade begins as an ordinary construction worker, so he does not start with guns or a sword. He gains access to them as the story and threats escalate.

Interface Scope Decisions View shipped and deferred interface work

Neon Bar

Enemies and crates dropped Neon orbs. Collecting these orbs filled the Neon bar. Once the bar was full, the player could activate the mech suit.

The functional Neon bar shipped with the game. We deferred its animated liquid presentation to save development time and meet the release date.

Codex (Deferred)

Codex was a planned pause-menu system for collecting audio logs that revealed more of the game’s backstory. We deferred it to keep the release on schedule.

Mech HUD (Deferred)

We planned the Mech HUD as a separate interface shown while the player controlled the mech. Leaving the mech would have restored the human HUD. We deferred the Mech HUD as a stretch goal.

Accessibility settings were planned during development but deferred so the team could meet the June 19 release deadline.

Trello Workflow

I spent more than 20 hours researching, building, and adjusting the Trello automations.

I created the development cards and tracked each assigned task daily. I also helped create art cards when I was responsible for supervising or tracking the work. Each card used the appropriate Art or Dev label and priority.

The automations used each card’s label, checklist state, and board location to keep the art and development workflows consistent.

Development workflow

Each development card moved from To Do to In Progress with the Dev label and a priority. Trello then added the Dev Progress checklist.

View the full development workflow
  1. To Do
  2. In Progress
  3. Testing (Dev)
  4. Completed if the pull request was approved and merged
  5. Debugging (Dev) if the technical director requested changes

When the work was ready, the card moved to Testing (Dev), where the technical director reviewed the pull request. If the technical director approved and merged it, I checked Finished. The automation then moved the card to Completed.

If the technical director left feedback that required more work, I selected the corresponding checklist item. The automation then moved the card to Debugging (Dev).

Automation rules

The automations watched each card’s label, checklist state, and board location. They added and updated checklists and moved cards through the correct review and testing stages.

This reduced the amount of manual Trello work needed to keep each card current.

HUD Redesign

Why I Redesigned the HUD

Problem
The original HUD placed information in several corners of the screen, covering parts of the platforms and pulling attention away from the action.
Direction
I wanted the main player information grouped in one place. I decided how the HUD should be organized and gave the Neon Steel art team feedback and direction.
Art and implementation
The Neon Steel art team created the final visual design. I built it in Unreal Engine with Blueprints.
Result
The final layout leaves more of the play space visible.
