Project overview

A puzzle game where the controls work against you

Rogue Malfunction is an isometric puzzle adventure about a robot trying to escape its creator, the Overlord. As the robot moves through the facility, the Overlord interferes by changing how its directional controls behave.

Each room asks the player to understand the new input mapping, navigate environmental hazards, solve the puzzle, and reach the exit. I created the central concept because I wanted the game jam theme, Going Rogue, to affect how the game played instead of existing only in the story.

Control remapping

Keeping a shared foundation without blocking the team

We initially planned to use one player controller across every level. That became impractical once three programmers needed different movement and balancing changes at the same time. It also created a clear risk of frequent merge conflicts.

We replaced that approach with a shared master controller foundation. Each programmer created a level specific controller from that base and tuned it for their puzzle while keeping the common movement behaviour consistent.

My Sequence level used faster and more demanding parkour, so I increased its movement speed to give players a better chance of completing the jumps. The remapping itself remained authored for each room rather than random or procedurally generated.

Game jam and playable content

Four puzzle rooms built into one release

The original jam ran from December 27 to December 31, 2025. We continued working afterward and combined all four puzzle levels into one release by February 16, 2026.

The final build includes Ethan Ticar's Three B's Puzzle, Phillip's Sokoban and Tiles puzzles, and my Sequence Puzzle. Together they cover movement remapping, jumping, parkour, door sequences, object puzzles, tile navigation, hazards, interaction, and level specific Overlord voice work.

Alice created the UI artwork and the robot model and rig. Animation integration problems prevented us from using the model as the playable character, so we reduced the scope and placed it in the opening environment instead of delaying the release.

Playtesting and iteration

Adjusting the Sequence level without removing its challenge

Playtesters repeatedly identified the Sequence level as too difficult. I revised its layout three times, shortened several jump distances, and increased the movement speed for that room.

The level was still intended to be demanding, so I added a clear Extremely Hard label before entry. Once players knew what to expect, I stopped receiving complaints about its difficulty.

Post jam updates also addressed progression bugs and edge cases while adding a pause menu, control reference, improved settings, updated menu presentation, button animations, lighting refinements, level selection, and level specific voice work.

Retrospective

What I would change next time

Development workflow

I would establish protected main and development branches with separate feature branches from the beginning. That would make collaboration between three programmers more predictable and reduce integration problems.

Level cohesion

I would plan stronger connections between the four rooms so their environments gradually reveal more about the robot, the Overlord, and the reason for the escape.

The strongest lesson was that communicating difficulty can matter as much as adjusting it. The Sequence level remained deliberately challenging, but setting that expectation changed how players approached it.
